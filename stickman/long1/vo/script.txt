In 1999, two students tried to sell their little search engine for seven hundred fifty thousand dollars.
The buyer said no. That search engine was Google. And today, its parent company is worth about three point six trillion dollars.
That's number one on this list. But before we get there, here are five other money decisions so bad, they almost hurt to watch.
This is StickWise. Let's count them down.
Number six. The billion dollar pizza.
May 2010. Bitcoin is brand new, and nobody really knows what it's for.
A programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz posts on a forum: I'll pay ten thousand bitcoins for a couple of pizzas.
A few days later, someone takes the deal. Two pizzas. Total value back then? About forty-one dollars.
Then Bitcoin went… a little crazy. At its peak in October 2025, those ten thousand coins were worth over one point two billion dollars.
And Laszlo? He says he doesn't regret it. Someone had to prove Bitcoin could buy real things. May twenty-second is now called Bitcoin Pizza Day.
But one point two billion is honestly nothing compared to number five.
Number five. The company that invented its own killer.
Kodak was a giant. At its peak, it was worth around thirty-one billion dollars, and it once employed over a hundred forty thousand people.
But back in 1975, a young Kodak engineer named Steve Sasson built something strange. The first digital camera.
It weighed eight pounds. It took twenty-three seconds to save one black and white photo, onto a cassette tape.
His bosses' reaction, as Sasson tells it? That's cute. But don't tell anyone about it.
The problem was simple. Kodak made a fortune selling film. And digital cameras don't need film.
Kodak did go digital eventually. But everyone else moved faster. Phones got cameras. Film faded away.
In January 2012, Kodak filed for bankruptcy. The company that invented the digital camera was beaten by it.
And yet, Kodak at least tried. Number four had a forty billion dollar offer on the table, and said no.
Number four. Yahoo says no to Microsoft.
February 2008. Microsoft wants to buy Yahoo. The offer? Thirty-one dollars a share. About forty-four point six billion dollars.
Yahoo's board looks at it and says: that undervalues us. No thanks.
Microsoft walks away. And over the next few years, Yahoo slowly fades. Google owns search. Facebook owns social.
In 2017, Verizon buys Yahoo's main business. The price? About four and a half billion dollars.
That's roughly ten percent of what Microsoft offered. Ninety percent of the value, gone, while they waited for something better.
Lesson: sometimes the best offer is the one already in your hand.

Okay. Number three. Blockbuster laughs at Netflix.
The year 2000. Blockbuster has about nine thousand stores around the world. It's the king of movie night.
Netflix is a tiny startup mailing DVDs in red envelopes. And it's losing money.
So Netflix's founders fly to Dallas, and offer to sell the whole company to Blockbuster. Price: fifty million dollars.
According to co-founder Marc Randolph, Blockbuster's CEO was trying not to laugh. Too niche, he said.
You know how this ends. Streaming happens. In 2010, Blockbuster goes bankrupt.
Netflix today is worth around two hundred sixty-five billion dollars. And there's exactly one Blockbuster store left, in Bend, Oregon.
But at least Blockbuster never owned Netflix. Number two actually owned a piece of Apple… and gave it back.
Number two. The third founder of Apple.
1976. Everyone knows Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak. But Apple actually had a third founder: Ron Wayne.
Wayne was older and more experienced. He wrote the partnership agreement and drew Apple's very first logo. His share: ten percent.
Then, twelve days later, he walked away.
The two Steves had almost nothing to lose. Ron had a house, a car, and savings. If Apple failed and owed money, creditors could come after him.
So he gave up his ten percent for eight hundred dollars. Later, he took fifteen hundred more to drop any future claim.
Today, that ten percent would be worth around four hundred billion dollars, before dilution.
And here's the twist. Now in his nineties, Wayne says he has no regrets. For him, the risk just wasn't worth it.
Which brings us to number one. The deal that could have been worth trillions.
Number one. The day Excite didn't buy Google.
1999. The internet is exploding, and Excite is one of the biggest search sites around.
Meanwhile, two Stanford students, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, have a search engine they think is better. And they're willing to sell it.
Investor Vinod Khosla pitches the deal to Excite's CEO, George Bell. First price: one million dollars.
Excite says no. So Khosla talks the founders down. Seven hundred fifty thousand dollars.
Excite says no again.
Bell later said it wasn't really about the money. Page wanted Excite to replace its own search technology with Google's. That was the deal breaker.
Two years later, Excite's parent company goes bankrupt.
And Google? Its parent company Alphabet is now worth around three point six trillion dollars. All from a seven hundred fifty thousand dollar no.
So what do all six have in common? Every time, someone chose what felt safe, or what felt like enough, right now.
A pizza today. Film profits today. A better offer someday. Eight hundred dollars today.
Nobody can see the future. But before every big money decision, you can ask one question: what could this become if I wait?
